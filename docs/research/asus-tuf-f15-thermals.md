# Your TUF F15 Is Thermally Throttling, Not Choked by Linux

The metrics measured on this machine are abnormal. A healthy FX507 chassis with an RTX 3050 sustains **~95 W at 1935–1954 MHz and 77–81 °C** over 30 minutes of continuous gaming, while CPUs in this chassis family operate around **69–80 °C under gaming loads** ([LaptopMedia](https://laptopmedia.com/review/asus-tuf-gaming-f15-fx507-review-there-are-too-many-versions/p7/); [Notebookcheck FX507ZM](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Asus-TUF-Gaming-F15-FX507ZM-laptop-review-Peak-GeForce-RTX-3060-performance.609347.0.html)). In contrast, this unit exhibits CPU temperatures of 94–98 °C at only ~55% utilization, alongside a GPU reaching 88 °C while throttled down to 36–40 W and 1230–1300 MHz: significantly hotter despite drawing less than half the rated power. This thermal profile indicates severely degraded heat dissipation from the chassis: obstructed air intakes, dust clogging the fin stacks, or dried thermal paste. The issue is not caused by Linux software power caps. Diagnostic telemetry rules out virtually all Linux-specific power limit hypotheses: `nvidia-smi` confirms a 95 W ceiling (80 W base + 15 W Dynamic Boost, identical to the Windows configuration), `nvidia-powerd` is running actively, and lowering CPU PL1/PL2 thresholds from 80/95 W to 45/65 W induced zero change in operating temperatures or frequencies. This lack of response is conclusive: if the CPU were power-constrained, reducing power targets would have lowered temperatures. Because thermal behavior remained static, the CPU was already drawing power well below those caps and still reaching 94–98 °C under moderate load. Remediation must prioritize hardware fundamentals: elevating the chassis rear or deploying an active cooling pad, clearing fan/fin debris, and repasting the dies. Software controls provide secondary mitigation: frame rate caps, DLSS, conservative EPP settings or disabling turbo, and disabling `game-performance`.

## An RTX 3050 at 88 °C and 40 W Delivers Less Than Half Expected Performance

ASUS equips the RTX 3050 in the FX507ZC/ZC4 with its maximum mobile power budget: **80 W base TGP + 15 W Dynamic Boost = 95 W**, featuring a 1740 MHz boost clock with a +50 MHz factory overclock ([ASUS ROG 2022 TGP Table](https://rog.asus.com/articles/rog-gaming-laptops/the-complete-list-of-geforce-gpu-tgp-for-2022-rog-tuf-laptops/)). Under LaptopMedia's stress testing, this GPU maintained **1935 MHz at 81 °C drawing 95 W** after 30 minutes. Reviewers noted that the cooling assembly operates near its limit even on healthy units ([LaptopMedia p7](https://laptopmedia.com/review/asus-tuf-gaming-f15-fx507-review-there-are-too-many-versions/p7/)). On this machine, the GPU operates at 55–60 W below rated TGP while running 7 °C hotter. Mobile NVIDIA GPUs enforce a strict thermal limit around **87 °C**, which the proprietary driver defends by throttling power draw ([NVIDIA Developer Forums](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/diff-windows-linux-target-temperature-87-c-80-c-after-reboot/333497)). The "SW thermal slowdown" flagged at 88 °C reflects this mechanism: the GPU draws low wattage not because software limits are missing, but because the kernel driver aggressively cuts power to prevent thermal runaway beyond the target limit.

Local system telemetry confirms that power budgets are configured properly. Running `nvidia-smi -q -d POWER` outputs a Default limit of 80 W with Current and Max limits set to 95 W, while `/proc/driver/nvidia/gpus/*/power` confirms "Notebook Dynamic Boost: Supported". According to NVIDIA documentation, when Dynamic Boost fails or is unconfigured, the maximum limit remains tied to the default value or displays "N/A". Here, it reports full capability ([NVIDIA Dynamic Boost README](https://download.nvidia.com/XFree86/Linux-x86_64/580.95.05/README/dynamicboost.html); [NVIDIA Developer Forums](https://forums.developer.nvidia.com/t/ubuntu-nvidia-smi-power-limit-stuck-at-95w/364197)). Furthermore, even if `nvidia-powerd` failed completely, the GPU would draw 80 W base TGP. Losing Dynamic Boost only accounts for a 15 W deficit, whereas this machine suffers an ~60 W shortfall. Systemd journals show recurring `JPAC is not created` and `Failed to get SysPwrLimitGetInfo` errors from `nvidia-powerd`. While undocumented publicly, these messages likely indicate failed system-wide AC/DC budget arbitration while GPU-side boosting remains armed. While worth tracking, they do not explain the 40 W thermal plateau.

## The CPU Heats the GPU via Shared Heatpipes

The FX507 cooling solution relies on **three shared copper heatpipes bridging both CPU and GPU**, a fourth dedicated heatpipe for VRAM and VRMs, three heatsink fin assemblies, and two radial fans. Unlike the RTX 3060/3070 variants, the RTX 3050 configuration **omits a dedicated secondary GPU heatpipe and individual radiator fin stack** ([LaptopMedia p8](https://laptopmedia.com/review/asus-tuf-gaming-f15-fx507-review-there-are-too-many-versions/p8/); [LaptopMedia p7](https://laptopmedia.com/review/asus-tuf-gaming-f15-fx507-review-there-are-too-many-versions/p7/)). Notebookcheck highlighted this bifurcation: due to reduced cooling hardware, different GPU tiers within the exact same chassis exhibit vastly different thermal margins ([Notebookcheck FX507ZC](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Asus-TUF-Gaming-F15-FX507ZC.623509.0.html)). When heatpipes are bridged, a CPU operating at 94–98 °C heat-soaks the thermal assembly, elevating ambient GPU junction temperatures and forcing the NVIDIA driver to curtail GPU power. This shared thermal bridge accounts directly for both observed symptoms simultaneously.

For baseline comparison, healthy CPUs in this chassis reach **~95 °C only under continuous 100% all-core AVX/Prime95 loads drawing 90–115 W**. On the FX507ZM, Prime95 reaches 95 °C for roughly 100 seconds at 115 W before stabilizing at **83 °C at 90 W** sustained ([Notebookcheck FX507ZM](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Asus-TUF-Gaming-F15-FX507ZM-laptop-review-Peak-GeForce-RTX-3060-performance.609347.0.html)). In gaming workloads under Turbo mode, Ultrabookreview measured CPU power at **~20 W with temperatures around 75–80 °C** ([Ultrabookreview](https://web.archive.org/web/2023/https://www.ultrabookreview.com/56362-asus-tuf-gaming-f15-review/)). Transient spikes to 95 °C during load transitions are within specification; sustained operation at 94–98 °C under ~55% gaming loads regardless of power limit clamps indicates severe dissipation failure. The i5-12500H features a **100 °C TjMax**, a 45 W base TDP, and a 95 W maximum turbo budget ([Notebookcheck i5-12500H](https://notebookcheck.info/Intel-Alder-Lake-P-i5-12500H-Notebook-Processor.677892.0.html)). At 94–98 °C, thermal throttling engages continuously.

To confirm thermal interface breakdown conclusively, actual in-game CPU package wattage should be verified via `turbostat` or MangoHud's `cpu_power`. If the package draws 25–40 W while sustaining 95 °C, conductive thermal transfer has failed. Conversely, if power exceeds 65 W despite configured 45/65 W caps, asusd PPT overrides are not binding. In asusd 6.x, PPT configurations apply only when their profile group is explicitly active, and custom TDP values require corresponding custom fan curves ([asusctl CHANGELOG](https://gitlab.com/asus-linux/asusctl/-/raw/main/CHANGELOG.md)). Active limits can be verified directly by reading `current_value` in `/sys/class/firmware-attributes/asus-armoury/attributes/ppt_pl1_spl/`.

## Windows Does Not Have a Cooler Secret Profile: Power Targets Are Identical or Higher

A widespread misconception assumes ASUS Armoury Crate on Windows operates with lower or cooler power profiles. Published data shows the opposite. For the FX507Z chassis, Notebookcheck recorded ASUS power profile targets under Windows ([Notebookcheck FX507ZM](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Asus-TUF-Gaming-F15-FX507ZM-laptop-review-Peak-GeForce-RTX-3060-performance.609347.0.html)), measured on the i7-12700H configuration:

| Armoury Crate Profile | CPU PL1 | CPU PL2 | Acoustic Target |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silent | 45 W | 60 W | <35 dB(A) |
| Performance | 70 W | 105 W | 40 dB(A) |
| Turbo | 90 W | 115 W | 48 dB(A) |
| Manual | 100 W | 135 W | 52 dB(A) |

Under Linux, the kernel 7.2 `asus-armoury` driver exposes a parameter space of **28–90 W for PL1, 28–135 W for PL2, and 5–15 W for Dynamic Boost** on the FX507Z. Default initialization assigns peak ceilings (90/135/15), mirroring Windows Turbo/Manual behavior. Neither `nv_temp_target` nor `nv_tgp` are exposed via firmware attributes ([LKML asus-armoury v17](https://lkml.iu.edu/2511.0/00952.html)). The local Performance profile was already operating **below** Turbo specifications (80/95/15) and was subsequently dialed down to 45/65 W (approaching Silent mode limits). Linux is not driving higher thermal loads than Windows. Operating differences center on architecture:

**Dynamic Boost.** Managed directly by the kernel driver in Windows, whereas Linux relies on the userspace `nvidia-powerd` daemon interfacing over systemd, D-Bus, and SBIOS calls. Notably, `nvidia-powerd` clamps CPU `scaling_max_freq` dynamically to yield thermal/power headroom to the GPU ([NVIDIA Dynamic Boost README](https://download.nvidia.com/XFree86/Linux-x86_64/580.95.05/README/dynamicboost.html)). Artificially suppressed CPU clocks during gaming without high CPU temperatures typically trace back to this daemon.

**Display MUX.** Operating in Hybrid mode (`gpu_mux_mode=1`) requires the discrete NVIDIA GPU to render frames while the Intel integrated GPU copies and presents them across the display bus, consuming CPU package power and adding thermal overhead. The `AsusMuxDgpu` profile links the display panel directly to the dGPU, bypassing PRIME copies at the cost of requiring a system reboot ([supergfxctl README](https://gitlab.com/asus-linux/supergfxctl/-/raw/main/README.md)).

**Cooling Fans.** In Windows Turbo mode, fans run at ~95% duty cycle; forcing 100% via Manual mode yielded negligible cooling differences ([Ultrabookreview](https://web.archive.org/web/2023/https://www.ultrabookreview.com/56362-asus-tuf-gaming-f15-review/)). Local fan curves configured for 100% duty cycle at 90 °C generate 5700/4700 RPM, confirming the fans are operating at their mechanical ceiling. Increasing fan profiles cannot overcome physical airflow starvation at the intake vents.

**Energy Performance Preference (EPP).** asusd links EPP values directly to active profiles (`platform_profile_linked_epp: true`), assigning `performance` to the Performance preset. This biases the CPU governor to ramp clocks aggressively whenever thermal headroom appears, identical to Windows Turbo heuristics.

Linux introduces two configuration hazards: `power-profiles-daemon` and `asusd` can trigger race conditions over EPP and `platform_profile` sysfs nodes if profiles are toggled via the KDE Plasma system tray without unified arbitration. A single daemon should manage power profiles exclusively. Additionally, CachyOS's `game-performance` script forces the system-wide `performance` profile for the duration of a gaming session ([CachyOS Gaming Wiki](https://wiki.cachyos.org/configuration/gaming/)), increasing thermal load counterproductively on thermally constrained hardware.

## Thermal Management Strategy: Chassis First, Software Second

The following prioritization ranks interventions by expected thermal impact based on empirical reviews and hardware telemetry:

| # | Action | Expected Impact | Evidence Baseline |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Elevate rear chassis by 2–5 cm or deploy an active cooling pad | −5 to −10 °C on CPU and GPU; eliminated GPU thermal throttling on FX507ZM | Empirical test on FX507ZM |
| 2 | Open chassis, clean dust from fans and heatsink fins, repaste CPU and GPU dies, verify VRAM/VRM pads | Highest remediation potential if thermal paste has degraded | Thermal engineering baseline |
| 3 | Apply frame rate limit below GPU saturation threshold (e.g., 60–75 FPS) | Substantial reduction in sustained GPU power draw and frametime variance | Documented GPU pacing behavior |
| 4 | Enable DLSS (Quality/Balanced) + NVIDIA Reflex | Lowers GPU render resolution and core utilization while maintaining visual fidelity | Standard engine behavior |
| 5 | Set EPP to `balance_performance`, or configure `no_turbo=1` in GPU-bound titles | Mitigates transient CPU wattage spikes, freeing shared heatpipe headroom | Shared thermal assembly physics |
| 6 | Disable `game-performance`; establish single EPP owner (`asusd`) | Prevents unconstrained boost ramping and daemon state conflicts | Architectural validation |
| 7 | Expand persistent NVIDIA shader cache; set `split_lock_mitigate=0` | Prevents runtime compilation stutter and Frostbite engine stalling | Documented driver heuristics |
| 8 | Benchmark `AsusMuxDgpu` and evaluate `NVreg_DynamicPowerManagement=0x01` if micro-stutter persists | Eliminates PRIME blit overhead and runtime D3 power state transition latency | Driver interface documentation |

Rear elevation offers immediate, verified gains. The stock FX507 rubber feet provide only **~2 mm** of bottom clearance, severely restricting intake airflow around the fans. Elevating the rear chassis 2–5 cm lowered internal operating temperatures by **up to 10 °C** in both Turbo and Performance modes. In Performance mode, the FX507ZM GPU went from severe 85–87 °C thermal throttling to sustaining **115 W continuous power** ([Ultrabookreview](https://web.archive.org/web/2023/https://www.ultrabookreview.com/56362-asus-tuf-gaming-f15-review/)). If elevating the chassis causes GPU power to climb from 40 W toward 70–80 W, airflow restriction is conclusively confirmed.

Thermal paste replacement targets the underlying degradation. ASUS factory documentation confirms that the 2022 TUF Gaming F15 utilizes **standard thermal paste on both CPU and GPU, not liquid metal** ([ASUS ROG TIM Guide](https://rog.asus.com/uk/articles/rog-gaming-laptops/celebrating-three-years-of-liquid-metal-in-rog-laptops/)), making manual repasting safe and standard. Chassis disassembly requires removing 12 Phillips screws (the lower-right screw is captive) ([LaptopMedia p8](https://laptopmedia.com/review/asus-tuf-gaming-f15-fx507-review-there-are-too-many-versions/p8/)). Cleaning the heatsink fin radiators of compacted dust blankets is critical, as repasting alone will not restore airflow if radiator exhaust channels remain obstructed. Factory thermal putty/pads on VRAM and VRM modules should remain untouched unless torn or degraded ([ASUS ZenTalk](https://zentalk.asus.com/t5/others/is-the-stock-thermal-paste-for-asus-tuf-f15-fx506hc-the-ptm7958/m-p/458792)).

On the software layer, **capping the frame rate** delivers the highest return. Under DX12 via vkd3d-proton, frame limiting must be set via `VKD3D_FRAME_RATE`; `DXVK_FRAME_RATE` and `DXVK_ASYNC` have no effect on D3D12 pipelines. Alternatively, MangoHud's `fps_limit` with `fps_limit_method=early` provides stable frame pacing ([vkd3d-proton](https://github.com/HansKristian-Work/vkd3d-proton); [MangoHud](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/flightlessmango/MangoHud/master/README.md)). Running multiple limiters concurrently introduces pacing jitter. Capping frames eliminates cyclic boosting and thermal throttling loops. Complementing the cap with **DLSS** via Proton's native DXVK-NVAPI layer reduces raw compute load, while NVIDIA Reflex minimizes input latency without requiring driver hacks ([NVIDIA Gaming Documentation](https://docs.nvidia.com/datacenter/tesla/driver-installation-guide/gaming.html)). Note that native Frame Generation is unsupported on RTX 30-series silicon.

CPU undervolting is hardware-locked on Intel 12th-Gen Alder Lake processors (MSR 0x150 lock) across both Windows and Linux ([Dell Support KB](https://www.dell.com/support/kbdoc/en-us/000211454/xps-15-9520-no-way-to-undervolt-the-computer-after-bios-v1-7-0?lang=en); [Manjaro Linux Forums](https://forum.manjaro.org/t/whether-intel-undervolt-is-no-longer-supported-values-do-not-equal-after-config/111890)). Because adjusting continuous power caps (PL1/PL2) did not alleviate thermal throttling, effective tuning must target transient clock peaks. Adjusting `profile_performance_epp` to `balance_performance` in `/etc/asusd/asusd.ron` or executing `echo 1 > /sys/devices/system/cpu/intel_pstate/no_turbo` in GPU-bound titles lowers CPU thermal dissipation, leaving critical thermal budget across the shared copper heatpipes for the GPU.

## Stutter Has Three Distinct Root Causes Isolated by MangoHud

Frametime anomalies stem from three independent mechanisms, each identifiable via distinct telemetry signatures:
1. **Shader compilation:** Appears as isolated single-frame spikes when new visual effects or shaders load, disappearing on subsequent executions.
2. **GPU thermal throttling:** Characterized by periodic framerate drops accompanied by `throttling_status` reporting Power/Temp and declining `gpu_core_clock`.
3. **CPU throttling & clock clamping:** Visible as frametime spikes correlating with CPU clock dips or `nvidia-powerd` reducing `scaling_max_freq`.

MangoHud logs these vectors to CSV when launched with `fps_metrics=avg+0.01+0.001`, `cpu_power`, `gpu_power`, and `throttling_status` enabled ([MangoHud](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/flightlessmango/MangoHud/master/README.md)). Benchmarking should isolate one variable at a time across identical test loops, discarding the initial run following driver or Proton updates.

To prevent shader pipeline stutter, ensure `~/.config/environment.d/gaming.conf` contains `__GL_SHADER_DISK_CACHE_SIZE=12000000000` and `__GL_SHADER_DISK_CACHE_SKIP_CLEANUP=1`, while disabling Steam's background shader pre-caching when running proton-cachyos or Proton-GE ([CachyOS Gaming Wiki](https://wiki.cachyos.org/configuration/gaming/)). vkd3d-proton maintains its internal pipeline cache directly inside the game prefix ([vkd3d-proton](https://github.com/HansKristian-Work/vkd3d-proton)). In Frostbite engine games (such as *Need for Speed Unbound*), kernel split-lock mitigations historically caused severe framerate collapses on Intel hybrid CPUs; verify that `sysctl kernel.split_lock_mitigate=0` is configured ([Valve Proton Issue #7561](https://github.com/ValveSoftware/Proton/issues/7561)).

For GPU power management, fine-grained Runtime D3 (`DynamicPowerManagement=3`) introduces enter/exit wake latencies ([NVIDIA RTD3 Documentation](https://download.nvidia.com/XFree86/Linux-x86_64/580.95.05/README/dynamicpowermanagement.html); [supergfxctl](https://gitlab.com/asus-linux/supergfxctl/-/raw/main/README.md)). If stutter occurs during initial launches, workspace switching, or menu navigation, test `options nvidia NVreg_DynamicPowerManagement=0x01` in `/etc/modprobe.d/`. For continuous micro-stutter in Hybrid mode, benchmark against `AsusMuxDgpu` to verify whether PRIME display blits are saturating the memory bus. In KDE Plasma Wayland settings, maintain Adaptive Sync on "Automatic" rather than "Always", as "Always" conflicts with KWin's triple-buffering logic ([KDE Discuss](https://discuss.kde.org/t/different-way-of-handling-always-variable-refresh-rate/16428)). Upstream NVIDIA Wayland pacing fixes included in Plasma 6.6+ resolve earlier compositing hitches ([Ubunlog](https://en.ubunlog.com/?p=57859)).

## Conclusion

System telemetry conclusively eliminates software misconfiguration as the root cause. Firmware power limits are correctly exposed, Dynamic Boost is active, and custom PPT caps produce zero thermal delta. The bottleneck is physical thermal dissipation. The RTX 3050 SKU of the FX507 chassis ships with the smallest heatsink surface area in the lineup and restrictive bottom intake clearances. With aged thermal paste or dust accumulation, the thermal assembly quickly crosses saturation thresholds where the CPU and GPU thermally choke each other across shared heatpipes. No software power curve can coax 95 W out of a GPU being throttled by driver safety limits to 40 W at 88 °C.

The decisive test is fast and non-invasive: elevate the rear chassis by 2–5 cm, run a 10-minute gaming loop with MangoHud active, and observe whether GPU power scales from 40 W toward 70–80 W while CPU temperatures retreat below 90 °C. If power scales, restricted intake airflow is confirmed and an active cooling stand will provide significant recovery. If thermal saturation persists, physical disassembly is required to clean the fin stacks and apply high-performance thermal paste. Once baseline heat dissipation is restored, software frame limiting, DLSS, and balanced EPP profiles will eliminate remaining frametime variance.
